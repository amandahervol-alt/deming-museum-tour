The Geode Kid Collection brings together geodes, thundereggs, petrified wood, fossils, and other minerals from across the region — many collected right here in Luna County and the surrounding area. So, what exactly is a geode? Picture a plain, unassuming rock — until you crack it open and find a hollow chamber lined with sparkling crystals. That's a geode: a spherical rock, formed inside sedimentary or volcanic stone, hiding a secret world of minerals inside. Those crystals grow when mineral-rich water seeps into small cavities (called vesicles) within volcanic rock and slowly fills them in. In other cases, a geode forms as older mineral deposits dissolve away, leaving a hollow that groundwater or hot fluids later begin to refill. Either way, the result is one of nature's most satisfying surprises. Some of the best geode hunting grounds nearby sit on private property, off-limits to visitors. But Rockhound State Park, just southeast of Deming, is a happy exception — one of the rare state parks in the country where you're welcome to take home what you find. Rockhounds here regularly turn up beautiful agates, and some get lucky and find thundereggs: solid, crystal-filled spheres that look like geodes but aren't quite the same thing. Many of the thundereggs on display in this museum were collected right here, in the Luna County and Mimbres Valley area. What is a thunderegg, exactly? Picture a round, unassuming nodule — usually about the size of a baseball, though they range from under an inch to more than three feet across — that forms inside layers of rhyolitic volcanic ash. Crack one open and you might find a center of chalcedony, fractured and re-decorated over time with bands of agate, jasper, or opal, sometimes all three at once. Quartz and gypsum crystals often show up too, along with other mineral surprises. From the outside, a thunderegg looks like an ordinary rock. Slice and polish one, though, and it can reveal patterns and colors unique to the very bed it came from — no two are ever quite alike. So how do a geode, a thunderegg, and an agate differ? A geode is defined by its hollow center, often lined with crystals. A thunderegg is a specific kind of formation — some thundereggs do have a hollow center and could technically be called geodes, but not every geode is a thunderegg, since hollows form in plenty of other ways too. And a thunderegg, in turn, is really just one particular form that agate can take. Nature, it turns out, has more than one way to make a masterpiece.
