About 5,000 Mimbres Indians lived along the Mimbres River and occupied the Deming, Silver City, and Lordsburg area—spanning Luna, Grant, and Hidalgo counties—from 950 to 1200 A.D. Fortunately, they buried pottery with their deceased under the floors of their homes. From archaeological excavations, we know the Mimbres were a deeply religious, agricultural people. Their painted pots depict wedding ceremonies, christenings, babies carried on cradleboards, children playing, and grandfathers telling stories. One famous bowl shows an elder with a cane and a hand on his back. Their pottery gives us extraordinary insight into their dress, hairstyles, weapons, ceremonial dancing, bird-snaring, and daily life. The circular hole in the center of burial pottery was ceremonial—known as 'killing' the pot to release the spirit of the vessel—often placed over the head of the deceased. Painted using chewed yucca leaf brushes and natural earth mineral pigments, Mimbres black-on-white and red-on-white pottery is internationally renowned. As archaeologist J.W. Fewkes observed, 'no Southwestern pottery, ancient or modern, surpasses that of the Mimbres.' Geographically, the Mimbres homeland was the only low-land route from the Atlantic to the Pacific without mountain navigation—the exact geographic reason why the southern transcontinental railroad and Interstate 10 were built right through Deming.
